[loc] The Arcology, the Chamber — 4 January 2051
N: In January of 2051 Albert Wesker stopped pretending that Umbrella was a company, and the narrator would like to record that almost nobody noticed, because the announcement was four pages of administrative prose and was made on a Tuesday.
N: What it said was this. Umbrella Life Sciences, Incorporated, a Delaware corporation, would continue to exist and would continue to file accounts and pay taxes and be regulated in every jurisdiction in which it operated. Nothing about it changed.
N: Above it, a new thing: the Umbrella Directorate, headquartered in the Free Territory, with responsibility for the territories, the protectorates, the Security Service, the Arcology's charter, and what the document called *the long programmes*.
N: Eleven directors. The narrator will name them, because this is the government that eventually runs forty worlds and it was drawn up in an afternoon.
[img] eleven figures seated around a long stone table in a high hall, formal, daylight
N: Albert Wesker, First Director. Catherine Adeyemi, Finance. Owen Carrick, Internal Security. Hale, the Security Service. Mr. Pell, the corporate estate. Dr. William Birkin, Structural Biology. Dr. Evelyn Marsh, the Progenitor Initiative. Sebastian Crane, Systems. The Red Queen, Modelling — and the narrator notes that her chair was a real chair, at the table, and that the vote to seat her was nine to one with one abstention and the one against was Carrick.
N: Two seats were left empty. Wesker never explained them. Catherine believed they were for successors; Carrick believed they were for the two people Wesker expected to have to buy; the Red Queen, asked directly in 2063, said *I have modelled it four thousand times and the answer that fits best is that he did not want a table that was full,* which is the only time she is recorded as guessing.
[img] {CARRICK} standing alone at a window in a stone hall, arms folded, grey sea beyond
N: Carrick's objection is on the record. It is four sentences long and he read it aloud.
CARRICK: (slow) Nine of the eleven people at this table cannot disobey the First Director, or are machines, or are things he made. The two who can are a woman who has told him no once in twenty-four years and a man who has told him no five times and been overruled five times.
CARRICK: (slow) This is not a government. It is Albert Wesker with a table in front of him.
CARRICK: (slow) I am voting against, I am recording my reasons, and I am taking the post anyway, because the alternative is that somebody worse takes it.
WESKER: (soft) Noted, Director Carrick. Minute it in full.
N: It was minuted in full. The minutes of the Umbrella Directorate were published quarterly from 2051 to 2091, unredacted, in eleven languages, and the narrator has read all of them, and that is why this account exists at all.

[loc] The Arcology — 2051 to 2058
N: The Directorate's first seven years are the least dramatic and most consequential period in this account, and the narrator will compress them into one page.
N: The protectorates went from one to nineteen. The mechanism was always the same: a small state in difficulty — climate, debt, a collapsed neighbour — asked for terms, and the terms were always better than anyone else's, and the sovereignty was always genuinely retained, and the referendums were always real and always won by margins over seventy per cent.
N: By 2058 the Umbrella Directorate was responsible for the defence, energy, health and currency of about nine million people across nineteen territories, none of which it had conquered, all of which had voted.
N: The Security Service went from six thousand to one hundred and ninety thousand and never fired a shot at a human being.
N: The Arcology went from sixty-one thousand people to four hundred thousand, and there was a waiting list of eleven million.
N: Raccoon City became, in 2054, the first city on earth where nobody paid for anything: not power, not water, not health, not education, not housing. Its citizens still worked, because there was work, and they still had money, because there was a currency, and the economists who came to study it wrote four hundred papers about what it meant and never agreed.
N: And the Points came in at a rate that made the entire first twenty years look like a rounding error.

[img] {CATHERINE} an elegant older woman in a charcoal suit sitting in a chair by a window, grey hair, evening
[loc] The Arcology, Catherine Adeyemi's apartment — 11 March 2059
N: Catherine Adeyemi turned seventy in 2059 and refused Aeterna for the twenty-second consecutive year.
N: She had refused it in 2037, when it became available to executives; in 2043, when it became free to every Umbrella employee; in 2049, when her doctor advised it; and every year since, in writing, to Pell's office, in a single line.
N: Wesker came to see her in March, which he did four times a year and which nobody else in the Directorate knew about.
WESKER: (soft) Your heart.
CATHERINE: (slow) My heart is seventy years old and has been arguing with you for thirty-two of them, which I imagine is the mechanism.
WESKER: (cold) The compound would repair it in eleven weeks.
CATHERINE: (slow) I know exactly what it would do, Albert. I have signed the accounts for it since it was a line item.
[pause 0.6]
CATHERINE: (slow) Do you want to know why? Properly, not the version I give Pell?
WESKER: (soft) I have wanted to know for twenty-two years and have never asked.
CATHERINE: (slow) Because somebody has to die.
N: The narrator will give her the page, because she has earned it and because this is the last time she speaks in this account.
CATHERINE: (slow) You've built a world where nobody has to. Nineteen territories, nine million people, free power and free medicine and a lineage on the ice that doesn't age at all. And every single person in it is going to spend the next two hundred years being *grateful*, because you gave them the one thing nobody can ever pay back.
CATHERINE: (slow) I was there at the beginning. I came to Wilmington in 2027 to short your stock and I stayed because you were the most interesting thing I had ever seen, and I have been complicit in every part of it, and I have argued with you a hundred and four times and won about thirty.
CATHERINE: (slow) When I am dead there will be no one left in this organisation who remembers a world that was not yours. Evelyn will not die. Birkin will not die. Hale and Pell were never alive in the way that matters. Carrick will take it eventually because his wife already has.
CATHERINE: (very slow) So I am going to die, on schedule, like a person. It is the only argument I have left and it is the only one you cannot win.
[pause 1.2]
N: Albert Wesker sat in a chair in an apartment above the Atlantic and did not say anything for a long time.
WESKER: (very soft) Yes.
CATHERINE: (slow) That's it? Thirty-two years and *yes*?
WESKER: (cold) You are correct. You have been correct on this since 2048 and I have known it since about 2050, and I have not found a way around it, and I do not expect to.
[pause 0.5]
WESKER: (soft) I would like you to know that when it happens I will not replace you. The chair will stay empty. There will be two empty chairs at that table and nobody will be told why, and in a hundred years somebody will write that the First Director was sentimental about an accountant.
CATHERINE: (slow) And will they be right?
WESKER: (very cold) They will be right that I have never once been able to replace the only person in the building who could tell me I was wrong about money and be listened to.
N: Catherine Adeyemi died on the fourth of November 2061, aged seventy-two, of an ordinary heart failure, in a city in the middle of an ocean that she had signed the accounts for.
N: Wesker attended the funeral. He did not speak at it. There is a photograph of him standing at the back in a black coat, and it is the only photograph of Albert Wesker in which he is not the subject of the frame.
N: The chair stayed empty for thirty years.
