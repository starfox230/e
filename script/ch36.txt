[loc] Hangzhou — 11 September 2077
N: Wen Shulan was ninety-nine years old in 2077. She had retired from the Ministry of State Security in 2061, had taken Aeterna in 2065 at the insistence of her daughter, and had spent sixteen years in a house outside Hangzhou writing a history that she did not intend to publish.
N: She had not been consulted about the Coalition. Her country had not joined it; that had been her doing, from retirement, in four conversations over two years, and it is the reason China was one of the two abstentions on the fifteenth of March.
N: In September Albert Wesker came to see her, and it was the fifth time they had met in forty-one years.
WEN: (slow) You look precisely the same.
WESKER: (soft) You look better than in 2036.
WEN: (slow) I am better than in 2036. That is your doing and I have never thanked you for it and I do not intend to start.
N: She poured tea. The narrator notes that neither of them had said anything about the Eleven Days for four minutes, which was, for both of them, a form of politeness.
WEN: (slow) They should not have done it.
WESKER: (cold) They were right to do it.
WEN: (slow) Both of those are true, which is the whole difficulty. I told four ministers in 2073 that it would fail and that the failure would make him unassailable, and I was told I was an old woman who had been compromised by a personal relationship.

WEN: (slow) I was an old woman who had watched you for forty-one years and had noticed that you have never once been beaten by anyone who came at you directly, and that on the four occasions you were beaten it was by someone who asked you for something.
N: She put the pot down.
WEN: (slow) So I am going to ask you for something.

N: What Wen Shulan proposed on the eleventh of September 2077 became, after two years and four months of negotiation, the Hangzhou Accord, and the narrator will give it to you in the four clauses that matter.
N: One. The Umbrella Directorate is recognised, by every signatory, as a legal person in international law with the capacity to hold territory, conclude treaties and be sued. Not a state. A new category, and the argument about what to call it lasted eleven months and was settled with the word *Authority*.
N: Two. Umbrella's core functions — medicine, energy, water, the strategic reserves — are placed under a standing obligation of supply. It may not withhold them from any population, for any reason, ever. The obligation is enforceable in a tribunal that Umbrella does not appoint and cannot veto.
N: Three. Every Umbrella territory retains the right to leave, by referendum, on two years' notice, and the signatories guarantee the independence of any that does.
N: Four — and this is Wen's clause, the one she wrote herself in a garden in Hangzhou, and the one that Albert Wesker argued about for nine months —
WEN: (slow) The Authority shall maintain no capacity to compel the obedience of any human being by means that the person cannot perceive, refuse or report. Existing personnel in that category shall be enumerated, registered, and progressively released.
[pause 1.0]
WESKER: (very cold) You are asking me to give up the Absolute.
WEN: (slow) I am asking you to stop *making* them, and to let the four hundred thousand you have made go, over whatever period is safe.
WESKER: (cold) Half of them will not survive it. You have read the Coalition's own analysis; you know what happened at Teraina in eleven days. These are not slaves I can free with a signature. They are people who have never once in their lives held their own reins, and some of them are forty years old.
WEN: (slow) I know. That is why the clause says *progressively*, and why there is no deadline in it, and why I have given you the right to set the pace.
[pause 0.8]
WEN: (very slow) Albert. I have watched you for forty-one years, and in all that time there is exactly one thing you have built that I would call evil, and it is not the virus, and it is not the soldiers, and it is not the monster in the stadium.
WEN: (slow) It is that you solved the oldest problem of power — that people can say no to you — and you solved it so completely that you have forgotten it was a problem worth having.
N: The narrator will record that Albert Wesker did not answer for a long time, and that what he finally said was not agreement.
WESKER: (very soft) If I sign that clause, I will spend forty years dismantling the thing that made all of it possible, and there is a real chance that it kills a great many of them, and the only reason I would do it is that a ninety-nine-year-old woman in a garden asked me to.
WEN: (slow) Yes.
WESKER: (cold) That is a very poor reason.
WEN: (slow) It is the only kind of reason anybody has ever had. You have been waiting since 2026 for somebody to be able to make you do something. Nobody can. So it will have to be somebody you *let*.
[pause 1.2]
N: He signed it in Hangzhou on the fourth of January 2080, with forty-one signatory states, in a room with four cameras in it.

N: The Registry's letter went to four hundred and eleven thousand people over thirty years, in the order in which their cohorts came up, and the narrator has read the fourth draft, which is the one that was used, and which Albert Wesker wrote himself in 2081 and rewrote three times.
N: It is nine paragraphs. It does not apologise, which was the most argued-about decision in the Directorate for a decade.
N: It says: that the recipient was placed into the world on a specific date, which is given; that their memories before that date were provided rather than lived; that everything since is entirely their own; that the people in their life are real and their feelings for them are real; that they are, in law and in every way the Directorate is able to determine, a person; that they have never been able to refuse an instruction from Umbrella and that this will end on a date, which is given; that they are entitled to see every record about themselves, and to compensation, and to leave with a pension at any time; and that the man responsible is named, and is alive, and will answer any letter they send him personally.
N: About sixty-one thousand of them wrote.
N: He answered all sixty-one thousand, over thirty-nine years, in longhand, at a rate of about nine a week, and the archive of those replies is four hundred volumes and is the other reason this account exists.
[img] a man in his fifties reading a handwritten letter at a kitchen table, plain room, daylight
N: Ray Dorsey — the plant supervisor from Chapter Four, who had telephoned his wife on his first Monday and told her he had been doing this for twenty-seven years — was in the second cohort, at the age of fifty-five, in 2082.
N: He wrote four lines. The reply is in volume nine.
MAN: (slow) *I don't want compensation and I don't want to leave. I want to know one thing. The holiday in Ocean City. Did it happen?*
WESKER: (slow) *Mr. Dorsey. No. It did not happen, and I am sorry to be the one to confirm it. You were given it complete, including the rain on the Thursday, on the eighth of January 2027, because a system I do not fully understand decided that a man of your age would have had one.*
WESKER: (slow) *I have thought about your question for some days. I think the honest answer is that the holiday is the only thing in your life that is not yours, and that everything you have done with your wife since that Monday is, and that there is rather a lot of it.*
WESKER: (very slow) *You are entitled to be angry with me and I will read anything further you send. — A. Wesker.*
N: Ray Dorsey took his wife to Ocean City in the spring of 2083. He wrote once more, two lines, and the reply in volume nine is one line, and the narrator will not print either of them, because there are four hundred volumes of this and some of it belongs to the people who wrote it.

N: The enumeration took four years. Four hundred and eleven thousand names.
N: The release programme took the rest of the century and is not finished at the end of this account. The narrator will give you its shape, because it is the only thing Albert Wesker ever did that he did badly, and he did it badly for twenty-seven years before it began to work.
N: The first cohort was nine hundred volunteers at Raccoon City in 2081: Absolute personnel in non-critical roles, released in the only way anyone could think of, which was that Wesker issued a final instruction saying *from this date you will take no instruction from me or from anyone in my name*, and then the Directorate waited to see what happened.
N: Fourteen of the nine hundred could not tolerate it. The narrator will not describe what that means; the medical literature calls it terminal executive vacancy and there are four papers about it and all of them are grim.
N: The other eight hundred and eighty-six were, within about nine months, indistinguishable from any other citizen of Raccoon City, and one of them opened a bakery, and one of them ran for the city council in 2086 and lost by four hundred votes and ran again.
N: Evelyn Marsh's group rebuilt the programme four times. The version that worked — from 2087 — was not medical at all. It was an apprenticeship: two years in which an Absolute employee was required, daily, to make one small decision that nobody had authorised, beginning with what to have for lunch.
N: The failure rate fell to under one per cent.

The sublevel did not exist until three o'clock on the Wednesday morning, and you were given the memory of cleaning it along with the keys, and I stood in the lobby on the Wednesday and watched you tell me about it and I knew, and I said nothing, and I have thought about that conversation for sixty-two years.
