N: And Adolf — the passenger, the other voice, the thing in his skull that was good at exactly one thing in all the world — was quiet for a long moment, and then began to talk, and he talked for two hours.
N: The narrator will give you the shape of it, because it is the only extended thing this voice ever says, and because everything Albert Wesker does in front of a crowd for the next sixty years is built out of it.
ADOLF: First: you will not use the five minutes.
WESKER: I have five minutes.
ADOLF: You have five minutes in which four other people are also speaking, and the room is at half attention, and the man before you will overrun. Every one of those four will use their time. You will use ninety seconds of yours, and you will say something that makes the other four sound like children, and then you will stop, and the silence will belong to you. A crowd does not remember who spoke longest. It remembers who was still there after the speaking stopped.
ADOLF: (slow) Second: you must give them something to be afraid of that is not you. They came to Basel to talk about access and pricing and supply. That is a room full of people arguing about how to divide a thing. Do not argue. Tell them what is coming for all of them, and be the only person in the building who is not surprised by it.
ADOLF: Third — and you will dislike this one — you must give something away. Not a discount. Something that costs you visibly, in public, that no one asked for and no one can match, and it must be announced in a flat voice as though it were an administrative detail. Generosity delivered as a *decision* is the most frightening thing a powerful man can perform, because it proves the power was never in doubt.
WESKER: (soft) And the pause.
ADOLF: (soft) The pause is everything. When you have given it to them, do not explain it. Do not soften it. Stand still and let four thousand people discover what they think while you watch them do it. You will want to fill that silence. That wanting is the thing you must kill.
