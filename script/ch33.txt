[loc] The world — 2064 to 2071
N: The narrator will now describe what happens to a species that stops getting old, and will try to do it without either of the two lies that are usually told about it.
N: The first lie is that it was a paradise. It was not.
N: The second lie is that it was a catastrophe. It was not that either.
N: What it was, was *enormous*, and slow, and unevenly distributed in ways that had nothing to do with the medicine and everything to do with roads.
N: By 2071, four point one billion people had taken Aeterna. Life expectancy at birth in the countries with functioning distribution went from eighty-one to a hundred and forty-one within a decade, and the projections kept moving because nobody had any idea where the ceiling was.
N: Population growth collapsed, not rose, which every demographer except four had got wrong: people who expect to live two hundred years do not have children at twenty-five. The global fertility rate fell below one point one by 2075 and the world's population began, for the first time since the fourteenth century, to fall.
N: Pension systems in nineteen countries failed outright and were rebuilt from nothing. The retirement age became a joke, then an insult, then a memory. Four hundred million people went back to education in a single decade.
N: And a generation of young people found every senior position on earth occupied by someone who was not going to vacate it, and the political consequences of that ran for forty years and were, the narrator notes, the single largest source of genuine hatred of Umbrella that ever existed.
[img] a street protest at dusk with banners and young people shouting, city buildings, tense
N: They called themselves the Term. Their founding argument was four words long and it was a good one: *nobody consented to this.*
N: They were not wrong. There had been no vote. Four point one billion people had individually chosen to take a medicine, which is not the same as a species choosing to change its relationship with death, and the difference between those two things is the entire subject of the next twenty years of political philosophy.
N: Wesker's response, in 2069, to a direct question from a Term delegation that he had personally invited to the Arcology, is on the record:
WESKER: (cold) You are correct. I did not ask you. There was no mechanism by which I could have asked you — there is no body on this planet with the authority to answer a question of that size, and if there had been, it would have taken forty years to convene and eleven million people a year would have died while it sat.
WESKER: (slow) So I did it, and I did it unilaterally, and it is irreversible, and I will not pretend that a distribution network is the same thing as consent.
WESKER: (very cold) What I will say is this: the alternative on the table was that four billion people continue to die on schedule in order to preserve a principle about procedure, and I have never met anyone willing to defend that sentence out loud with the dying person in the room.
N: The delegation's leader — a twenty-six-year-old from Lagos named Adaeze Kalu, who becomes, in twenty years, the most important person in this account who is not a Wesker or a Marsh — said the thing that Umbrella's own internal analysis had been saying for four years.
WOMAN: (slow) You keep telling us what the alternative was. You never tell us who gets to decide what the alternative is next time.
N: Wesker did not answer her.
N: The narrator notes that he did not answer her, that the Red Queen's minute of the meeting records a nine-second pause at that point, and that it is the longest recorded pause of his life outside a stage.

N: The narrator will give you the first year of free Aeterna as the people who administered it saw it, because the Assembly debates are about consent and the clinics were about queues.
N: Umbrella had built forty-one plants in secret over four years and had nine hundred million courses in warehouses on the day of the announcement. It was not enough. It was not remotely enough: there were seven billion people who had not had it and the manufacturing ran at about four hundred million courses a year.
N: Which meant an order, and the order was the most consequential administrative decision of the century, and Wesker did not make it.
N: He gave it to the World Health Organization — an institution that had spent thirty years being politely outperformed by Umbrella and that had, by 2064, almost no budget and enormous moral authority — and he gave it to them with one line of instruction and no oversight: *You will decide who is first. We will manufacture and deliver. We will not be consulted and we will not comment.*
N: They chose oldest-first, globally, without regard to nation, income or politics. It took nine years to clear the backlog. It was administered by four hundred thousand people in a hundred and ninety countries and there was, by the WHO's own audit in 2074, corruption in the distribution chain in about eleven per cent of districts and theft of about four per cent of stock.
N: Wesker was asked about the four per cent at a press conference in 2071 and gave an answer that is in every textbook on institutional design.
WESKER: (cold) Four per cent is theft. Ninety-six per cent is the highest delivery efficiency ever achieved by any humanitarian programme in history, by a margin of about thirty points.
WESKER: (slow) If Umbrella had run it, the theft would have been nearer nought point one, because we do not have elections or ministries or cousins. And the programme would have belonged to me, and in forty years there would have been no institution left on this planet capable of distributing anything at all.
WESKER: (very cold) I bought ninety-six per cent, and an institution that still exists. It was the cheapest thing I have ever paid four per cent for.
