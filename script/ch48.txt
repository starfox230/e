# 48 | The Two That Are Still Running

[loc] The Ochoa system — 19 March 2268
N: The ship that arrived at Ochoa in March of 2268 was not Veth, was not Compact, was not Choir, and was not anything in nine hundred light years of catalogue.
N: It was four kilometres long and it was welded. Human eyes could see that from the approach telemetry: four million hand welds, the ribs of a hull built in a yard by people arguing about a budget, and a drive geometry that the human sphere had been using for a hundred and seventy-six years and had believed nobody else had.
N: It broadcast on approach, in the clear, in a language nobody had heard, and the Compact's translation array had it in nine hours because the transmission carried its own primer, built the way a patient teacher builds one.
N: And the first sentence, when it came out, stopped four hundred worlds.
N: It was eleven words long, and every account of the next twenty years begins by quoting it.
[pause 0.6]
TESH: (slow) We are looking for the one who published the catalogue. We have one too.

[music] m_horror -9
[img] {WESKER} standing alone in a dim observation chamber watching a strange ship through a wide port
[loc] Dock One — 4 April 2268
N: Albert Wesker had been waiting for this conversation since the nineteenth of March 2051, when a panel in an empty chamber had told him there had been four hundred and eleven worlds, that four hundred and one had failed, and that of the nine that had not, *two are still running.*
N: He had asked what that meant for two hundred and seventeen years. He had been told the panel was accurate.
N: The species aboard that ship call themselves a word the array renders as the Ninth, which is not a name but a position in a list, and which they had taken from their own System's ledger, and which tells you most of what you need to know about them.
[img] a tall dignified alien figure in simple robes seated across a table from a man in a black coat
[loc] The neutral room, Dock One — 11 April 2268
N: Their host — the individual their System had chosen, three thousand one hundred years ago, and who had been alive ever since — was a person the record calls Tesh, of a species with four sexes and no equivalent word for *ownership*, and Tesh had spent three millennia building something that the human record has never been able to describe without sounding sarcastic.
N: Tesh had built a library.
TESH: (slow) You are much younger than I expected.
WESKER: (cold) Two hundred and forty-two years.
TESH: (slow) Yes. Very young. I was four hundred before I understood what the catalogue was doing to me, and eleven hundred before I stopped.
[pause 0.8]
TESH: (slow) You published it at one hundred and eighty-six. That is why we came. Our System showed us your world's ledger entry nine years ago — it does not usually show us other worlds; it showed us that one — and the entry said: *the interface has been published and the host was not killed.*
TESH: (very slow) In three thousand one hundred years I have never seen it volunteer anything.
N: The narrator wants to be exact about the next part, because it is the answer to the question this account has been asking since Chapter One.
WESKER: (very cold) What is it?
TESH: (slow) I do not know. I have asked for three thousand years in four hundred ways. I have built instruments. I have had a civilisation of nine billion people looking for it for two millennia. We have never located anything.
TESH: (slow) But I know what it *does*, because I have watched it do it to me, and because you have now published eleven thousand pages that match my own record almost exactly, in a different language, on a different world, four hundred years apart.
[pause 1.0]
TESH: (very slow) It is looking for something, and it has not found it, and it has been looking for a very long time.
WESKER: (soft) Looking for what?
TESH: (slow) Consider what it pays for. Not conquest — my world was never conquered; I bought nine hundred libraries and it paid me for every one. Not kindness; it paid the tyrants too, the record is explicit. It pays for *consequence*. For a will that makes a difference that would not otherwise have been made.
TESH: (slow) And consider what it does when the will stops mattering. It leaves.
TESH: (very slow) Four hundred and one worlds failed. Not because the host died — because the host *arrived*. Because he took the catalogue and built a thing so complete that nothing further would ever happen there, and the ledger went quiet, and the System closed the world and moved on.
[pause 1.2]
N: Albert Wesker sat very still in a room in orbit above the Earth.
WESKER: (very cold) It is not measuring achievement. It is measuring whether anything is still *happening*.
TESH: (slow) That is my conclusion after three thousand years. I could be wrong. I have been wrong about it four times.
WESKER: (soft) And the ninth world? Yours is the ninth in its list, and mine is the four hundred and eleventh. What happened to the other seven?
TESH: (very slow) They are still there. All seven. Perfect, stable, immortal, and nothing has happened in any of them for between four hundred and nine thousand years. I have visited three.
[pause 0.8]
TESH: (slow) They are the most beautiful places I have ever seen, and there is nobody home, in the sense that matters.

[loc] Dock One — April to September 2268
N: They talked for five months.
N: The narrator will give you the four things that came out of it, because between them they are the ending of this story.
N: The first: the Ninth and the human sphere exchanged everything. Not treaties — *records*. Three thousand one hundred years of Tesh's ledger against two hundred and forty-two of Wesker's, published in full on both sides, to every species in reach, in the clear.
N: The second: the Compact, which had been told about the catalogue in 2212 and had been quietly looking ever since, found four more. Not hosts — *former* worlds. Four of the four hundred and one failures, dead systems with impossible architecture in them, in a volume nobody had surveyed because there was nothing there to trade.
N: The third: Tesh stayed. Three thousand one hundred years old, and she settled on Ochoa, in a house, near a colony of nine hundred thousand people who argued constantly, and when asked why she said it was the first place she had been in two millennia where the outcome was not obvious.
N: And the fourth is a question that Albert Wesker asked in the last week, and the answer he got, and it is the reason he did what he does in the next chapter.
WESKER: (very cold) You have had three thousand one hundred years and you have never been offered a final panel.
TESH: (slow) No.
WESKER: (soft) I have one. It has been open since 2262. I have not read it.
[pause 1.0]
TESH: (very slow) Then it is finishing with you.
TESH: (slow) That is not a threat and I am not being poetic. In my record there are nine hosts who reached what you would call the sixth tier. Seven of them were offered something at the end that I have only ever seen described, because the descriptions stop.
TESH: (slow) It comes when the world no longer needs the host. Yours does not need you. Mine still needs me, which I have come to understand is not a compliment: it means I failed to build something that could continue without me, and you did not.
[pause 0.8]
WESKER: (very soft) What does it offer?
TESH: (very slow) I do not know, Albert Wesker. But I know what it costs, because it is written in the terms and neither of us has ever read them properly.
TESH: (slow) *What is given is given.* Everything it gave you stays. The cities, the medicine, the drive, the lineage, the nineteen billion people. It does not take those back.
TESH: (very slow) It only takes back the one thing it ever actually gave you, which was itself.
