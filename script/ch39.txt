[loc] The Umbrella Free Territory — 4 September 2089
N: Mira Halder was thirty-nine years old in 2089 and had been a citizen of nowhere since the day she was born.
N: The narrator introduced her in Chapter Twenty-Nine, in one line, as the first child born in the Arcology: a Bangladeshi structural engineer and a Peruvian marine biologist who had met in a canteen, and a hospital in the middle of an ocean.
N: This chapter is hers, and it is here because everything else in this account is told from inside the room where the decisions are made, and because a story told only from that room is a lie.
[img] a crowded canteen on an ocean platform, workers of many nationalities eating, big windows onto the sea
N: What it is like to be an Umbrella citizen, in 2089, from the inside:
N: You have never paid for anything. Not power, not water, not medicine, not your education, not the apartment you live in. You have a currency and you use it for the things people use currency for — a better coffee, a boat, a holiday on an actual continent — but the floor beneath you has never once moved.
N: You are thirty-nine and you look twenty-six, because you have been on Aeterna since you were eleven, along with everyone you know. Your parents are seventy-one and look forty. You have met exactly four people in your life who have died, and three of them chose to.
N: You have voted in nineteen elections for the Arcology's assembly, which controls the schools, the housing, the ports, the courts and about nine per cent of the budget. You have never voted for a First Director and neither has anybody else. The assembly has twice passed resolutions asking for that to change, and both times the Directorate has replied, politely, that the question is under review.
N: It has been under review since 2064.

MIRA: (slow) I want to be careful how I say this, because everybody from outside wants me to either denounce him or thank him, and I'm not going to do either.
MIRA: (slow) He is the reason my mother is alive. She had a cardiac thing at forty-four and the hospital here fixed it in an afternoon and it cost nobody anything. He's the reason I was born in a city that shouldn't exist, and the reason I've got a job building the second one, and the reason I'll probably see the year 2300.
[pause 0.6]
MIRA: (slow) And I have never once been asked whether I wanted any of it. Not once, in thirty-nine years, about anything that mattered. We get to vote on the ferry timetable.
N: She was, in 2089, the chief structures engineer on the Arcology's second platform, a hundred and forty kilometres south, which was being built the slow way — with contractors, with steel, over eleven years — because the assembly had asked for it to be built that way and the Directorate had, to everyone's surprise, agreed.
MIRA: (slow) That's the thing people outside don't understand about the argument here. We're not oppressed. Nobody's got a boot on us. It's that we're *comfortable*, permanently, and we didn't build it, and we can't lose it, and there's nothing any of us can ever do that will matter as much as one decision he makes on a Tuesday.
MIRA: (very slow) Do you know what the suicide rate is in the Free Territory? It's four times the world average, and it's all people between thirty and sixty, and the Directorate's own report says the cause is what it calls *consequence deficit*.
MIRA: (slow) We call it the Tuesday problem.

[loc] The Arcology Assembly — 19 September 2089
N: On the nineteenth of September 2089 the Arcology Assembly debated a motion, tabled by Mira Halder and eleven others, entitled *On the Question of Consent.*
N: It asked for four things: an elected First Director; a right of the territories to amend the Directorate's charter; the publication of the System's existence, whatever it was, in plain language; and a timetable.
N: It is the single most important document produced by anyone in this account who is not Albert Wesker, and it passed the Assembly by three hundred and eleven votes to nine.
N: He came to answer it himself, eleven days later, which nobody expected, and he took questions for four hours, which nobody had ever seen.
