N: Graham Tolliver had saved his company, and everybody had told him so.
N: The licence had cut his run time to thirty-one minutes. The trade press had called him decisive. His lender had stopped calling on Fridays. And every quarter, nine million dollars left his accounts and went to Wilmington, and four per cent of every panel he sold went with it, and at the end of the first quarter his chief financial officer put a slide in front of him showing the five-year projection, which was a straight line going down at a polite angle.
N: It is one thing to lose. It is another thing entirely to be kept.
N: On the sixth of April, Tolliver stopped negotiating with Albert Wesker and started fighting him, and the narrator would like to say, before any of this begins, that he was not a fool. He was a competent chief executive with eleven hundred employees who depended on him, and every single thing he tried was the correct thing to try.
[img] a busy corporate legal office, documents in stacks, lawyers working at a long table, daylight
N: He tried three moves in eleven days.
N: The first was legal: a suit in the District of Delaware alleging that the archive licence Verdant Axis had bought in 2019 had been exclusive in substance if not in form, and that Umbrella's entire instrument line was built on material it had no right to commercialise. It was a stretch. It was also the kind of stretch that takes two years and eleven million dollars to unstretch.
N: The second was people: offers to Evelyn Marsh and to two of Voss's process engineers, at two and a half times their salaries, with signing bonuses and titles.
N: The third was quieter. He hired a firm in Virginia — the kind with a two-word name and no website — to find out what was in the building in Wilmington. What he said, on a call that was later read into evidence in a hearing that went nowhere, was: *I don't want dirt. I want to know how they did it in ninety days. Because nobody does it in ninety days.*
N: And that, of the three, was the one that ended him.

N: The proposal was simple. Merrick Trust and the syndicate would support a recapitalisation in which Umbrella Life Sciences would acquire Verdant Axis Therapeutics for a dollar a share, assume the debt, retain the workforce, and drop the litigation. The alternative was to test the covenant in July, which the company would fail.
N: Nine directors. They had been arguing for fifty minutes when the door at the end of the room opened and Wesker came in alone, twenty minutes after he had been due, and sat down in the empty chair without apologising.
N: He let them finish. That is the thing that the two surviving directors both mentioned, independently, years apart, to two different people. He let them finish arguing, for six more minutes, about a company he had already bought.
TOLLIVER: (fast) You've had this planned since November.
WESKER: Since the twelfth of November, yes.
TOLLIVER: You gave me a licence so I'd survive long enough to be worth taking.
WESKER: I gave you a licence so that you would spend six months proving, in public, with audited numbers, that my chemistry is worth nine million a quarter to a competitor. You have been my best salesman since Christmas. It would have been discourteous to interrupt you.
N: Tolliver stood up. He was not a small man, and he had been a serious person for thirty years, and the narrator would like to record that what he said next was the truest thing anyone said in that room.
