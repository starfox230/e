N: An industry does not fall in a day. It falls the way a building falls: for eleven years nothing happens, and then everything happens at once, and afterwards everyone says they saw it coming.
N: By 2039 the global pharmaceutical industry consisted of Umbrella and nine survivors.
N: Umbrella had not fought any of them. That is the part that is hard to convey. There had been no price war, no litigation campaign, no hostile bids after Verdant Axis in 2027. What there had been was this: a company that gave away its diagnostic platform, sold the only functioning longevity therapy on earth, held the medical reserves of nine governments at its own cost, and could bring a new molecule from genome to manufacture in four days when the industry standard was four years.
N: You cannot compete with that. You can only be acquired by it, or become a supplier to it, or leave.

N: Umbrella announced, in April of 2039, a thing it called the Open Pipeline: any pharmaceutical company on earth could submit a molecule to Umbrella's discovery and manufacturing infrastructure, receive in return a full development package — synthesis route, toxicology, clinical design, regulatory dossier — and keep one hundred per cent of the product, in exchange for a four per cent royalty and the right for Umbrella to manufacture.
N: It was, on its face, the most generous offer in the history of the industry.
N: It was also the end of the industry, and every chief executive on earth knew it within a week, and every single one of them signed anyway, because the first one to refuse would spend four years developing a molecule that their competitor would have on the market in eleven months.
[img] a montage of newspaper business pages and stock tickers, headlines about mergers
N: By December of 2039, six hundred and eleven pharmaceutical companies had joined the Open Pipeline, including seven of the nine survivors.
N: By 2041 the industry's research divisions had quietly ceased to exist — not closed, just *unnecessary* — and their staff had been hired by Umbrella, at their own request, in numbers that eventually reached two hundred and eighty thousand.
N: Umbrella never bought a single one of them. It simply became the thing they all ran on, and then it was the industry, and there was no moment anyone could point at.

MAN: (slow) They did not close us. That is what people misunderstand. Our laboratory is open, our salaries are paid, our coffee is good. We simply stopped being asked.
MAN: (slow) In 2038 we had four hundred projects. In 2041 we have nine, and all nine are things Umbrella's own pipeline flagged as not worth their time, and we know it, because the flag is in the file they send us.
MAN: (very slow) I have been given everything I asked for my entire career: funding, staff, equipment, freedom. And I have not had an idea that mattered since I was forty-four, and I cannot tell any more whether that is me or whether it is the arrangement.
